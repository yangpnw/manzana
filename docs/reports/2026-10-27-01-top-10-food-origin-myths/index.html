🌍 Top 10 Food Origin Myths

Oct 27, 2026

1. 🇲🇽 Caesar salad isn't named after Julius Caesar

A popular myth says the Roman emperor Julius Caesar is behind it. The real Caesar was Caesar Cardini, an Italian immigrant who ran a restaurant called Caesar's in Tijuana, Mexico. Americans crossed the border to eat there because alcohol was banned back home.

His daughter Rosa said it happened on July 4, 1924. A holiday rush used up most of the kitchen's food, so Cardini threw together what was left and tossed it right at the table to put on a show.

The first version used whole lettuce leaves you picked up with your fingers, and Worcestershire sauce instead of anchovies.

It's named after the cook, not the emperor.

2. 🏴󠁧󠁢󠁥󠁮󠁧󠁿 The sandwich's card-table origin story started as a rumor

The story goes like this: the Earl of Sandwich, an English lord, didn't want to stop playing cards, so he asked for roast beef between two slices of toast. No fork needed, and no greasy cards.

But the tale first shows up in a 1770 travel book by a French writer, Pierre-Jean Grosley, who spent a year in London. The Earl's biographer thinks he was more likely eating at his desk, because he was so busy with the navy, politics and the arts.

And people were putting food in bread long before him. More than 2,000 years ago, a Jewish teacher named Hillel wrapped lamb and bitter herbs in flatbread, like a wrap.

Basically, the Earl got his name on the sandwich, but not the idea.

3. 🇮🇹 Marco Polo didn't bring pasta to Italy from China

Many historians call this one a myth. In 1154, a geography book written for the king of Sicily already described noodles being made on the island and shipped abroad. That's over 100 years before Marco Polo set off for China.

Noodles most likely traveled the long way around: from China along the Silk Road trade routes to Persia, then with Arab traders to Sicily and Spain.

So where did the Marco Polo story come from? The food writer Jane Grigson believed it began in the 1920s or 1930s, in an ad for a Canadian spaghetti company.

Think of it as a marketing slogan that ended up in history books.

4. 🇺🇸 Potato chips weren't invented to annoy a picky customer

The legend: in 1853, at a restaurant in Saratoga Springs, New York, a cook named George Crum got fed up with a customer who kept sending back his fries for being too thick. So Crum sliced potatoes paper-thin, fried them crisp and piled on salt, and the customer loved them.

It's a fun story, but it's known to be untrue. The oldest known chip recipe is in an English cookbook from 1817, 36 years earlier. A later edition says to peel the potato "round and round, as you would peel a lemon," then fry the shavings in fat.

A packaging company spread the Crum version in a big 1973 ad campaign.

In other words, the snack is older than the story about its birth.

5. 🇮🇹 Pizza Margherita probably wasn't made for a queen

The legend says that on June 10, 1889, a Naples pizza maker named Raffaele Esposito made three pizzas for the visiting Queen Margherita. She loved the one in Italy's flag colors: red tomato, white mozzarella and green basil. A thank-you letter from the palace still hangs in his old shop, now Pizzeria Brandi.

Here's the problem. Researchers doubt the letter is real, no newspaper from the time mentions the visit, and the story and the name were first pushed in the 1930s and 1940s.

Pizza toppings like tomato, basil and thin slices of mozzarella were already written about in Naples in 1849, 40 years before the queen's supposed dinner.

It's a royal stamp of approval added after the fact.

6. 🇯🇵 Fortune cookies started out Japanese, not Chinese

You get them at Chinese restaurants, but they're not from China. In the 1800s, bakers in Kyoto, Japan, made a similar cookie called tsujiura senbei (辻占煎餅). It was bigger and darker, made with sesame and miso, and the paper fortune was tucked into the fold instead of inside.

Japanese immigrants in California most likely brought the idea to the US in the early 1900s. Then, during World War II, the US government forced over 100,000 Japanese Americans into guarded camps, including the people who made the cookies. One theory is that this gave Chinese American bakers their chance to take over.

Today about 3 billion fortune cookies are made every year, mostly eaten in the US.

Basically, a Japanese snack that ended up working at Chinese restaurants.

7. 🇨🇳 Chop suey wasn't invented for a visiting Chinese leader

One story says that when the Chinese statesman Li Hongzhang visited the US in 1896, a cook made up chop suey to please both Chinese and American tastes. But a scholar who dug into the records found no proof that Li ate chop suey there at all. Li even brought three of his own chefs.

The dish was already in a Brooklyn newspaper in 1884, 12 years before his trip. Its name likely comes from 杂碎 (zá suì, "tsap seui" in the Taishan area of Guangdong), meaning "odds and ends," a dish from the home region of many early Chinese immigrants.

Restaurant owners were probably just smart: they used the famous visit to advertise chop suey as Li's favorite.

Think of it as a famous guest's name borrowed for a menu.

8. 🇫🇷 Dom Pérignon didn't invent bubbly champagne

Dom Pierre Pérignon was a French monk who ran the wine cellar of an abbey in the Champagne region from 1668 until 1715. Back then, bubbles were a nightmare. When the weather warmed up in spring, the wine started fizzing again inside the bottles, and they could explode. One blast could set off the bottles next to it.

So Pérignon actually tried to stop the bubbles. Bubbly champagne didn't become the main style until the mid-1800s, over 100 years after he died.

His famous line, "Come quickly, I am tasting the stars!", first shows up in a printed ad in the late 1800s.

In other words, the monk famous for bubbly spent his career fighting bubbles.

9. 🇪🇹 The goatherd who discovered coffee probably never existed

The legend says a goat herder named Kaldi noticed his goats getting super hyper after nibbling red berries from a bush. He tried them too, then took them to a monk, who threw them in the fire. The smell was so good that they made a drink from the roasted berries.

The story supposedly happened around the year 850, but it first appears in a book printed in Rome in 1671, more than 800 years later. That first version doesn't even give the herder a name. The name "Kaldi" seems to have been added in the 1900s.

Even so, Ethiopia's biggest coffee chain today is called Kaldi's Coffee.

It's a mascot that turned into a history lesson.

10. 🇺🇸 The chocolate chip cookie probably wasn't an accident

The famous story: in the 1930s, Ruth Wakefield, who ran the Toll House Inn in Massachusetts, ran out of nuts or hoped the chocolate would melt in the oven, so she tossed in chopped chocolate instead. Surprise, a new cookie!

Food writers today think that's unlikely. Wakefield had studied home economics and ran a very organized kitchen. In the 1970s she said she simply wanted to give guests something new, so she broke up a Nestlé chocolate bar with an ice pick and stirred the pieces into her dough.

Nestlé later printed her recipe on its chocolate packages. Wakefield said she was promised just one dollar for it.

Not a happy accident, just a cook who knew exactly what she was doing.

Fun fact

In 1983, San Francisco and Los Angeles took their fight over who invented the fortune cookie to San Francisco's Court of Historical Review. During the hearing, someone handed in a cookie as evidence with this message inside: "S.F. Judge who rules for L.A. Not Very Smart Cookie." The judge ruled for San Francisco, and Los Angeles officially complained. Courts have argued over food many times, like the Massachusetts court that decided in 2006 that a burrito is not a sandwich, so strange food trials could be a great next rabbit hole.
