🌍 Top 10 Daring US Construction Feats

Nov 14, 2026

1. 🏨 Chicago jacked up a hotel with the guests still inside

In the 1850s, Chicago sat barely higher than Lake Michigan, so its streets stayed swampy and dirty water spread disease. In 1854, cholera killed six percent of the city's people.

The fix: lay new sewers on top of the old streets, bury them under several feet of dirt, and lift most buildings up to the new ground level. Crews did it with jackscrews, big screw jacks turned by hand.

In 1861, 500 men working 1,000 jackscrews raised the six-story Tremont House hotel, and it stayed open the whole time. One guest noticed the front steps got steeper every day. By checkout, windows that had been at eye level were several feet above his head.

Basically, the whole hotel took the elevator.

2. 💥 The Hoosac Tunnel's diggers met inside a mountain, about half an inch off

The Hoosac Tunnel runs 4.75 miles (7.6 km) through a mountain in Massachusetts. Work started in 1851, and the first train rolled through in 1875, after the bill grew from $2 million to $21 million.

To speed things up, crews dug in from both ends and also from a shaft dropped 1,028 feet (313 m) straight down into the middle, nearly as deep as the Eiffel Tower is tall. They blasted the rock with nitroglycerin, its first big use in the United States.

Surveyors kept everyone on course with sighting towers on the mountaintop. In 1872, two of the tunnels met within 9/16 of an inch (1.4 cm). The price was terrible: 135 confirmed deaths, and workers called it the "Bloody Pit."

Picture two people digging toward each other through a mountain in the dark, and their shovels meeting.

3. 🚂 Railroad crews laid 10 miles of track in a single day

On April 28, 1869, Central Pacific crews in Utah laid 10 miles and 56 feet (about 16 km) of track in one day. It's a record that still stands.

It wasn't luck. The ground was already graded flat, five trainloads of rails and ties were lined up ready, and an army of workers was ready to go. The story goes that it settled a $10,000 bet.

Twelve days later, on May 10, 1869, the last spike was driven at Promontory Summit, joining the Central Pacific and Union Pacific into the first railroad across the country.

It's like paving the whole drive to the next town in one workday.

4. 🌉 Brooklyn Bridge workers dug the riverbed inside giant air-filled boxes

To hold up the bridge's towers, builders sank two huge upside-down wooden boxes called caissons to the bottom of the East River. Air was pumped in to keep water out, and men climbed down inside to dig away the riverbed so the box sank deeper.

On the Manhattan side the box went 78.5 feet (24 m) down, and the air inside was squeezed to 35 pounds per square inch, more than twice normal air pressure. Coming back up from that made workers sick with what the project doctor first named "caisson disease," which we now call the bends. In just four months of 1872 he treated 110 cases, and three workers died.

The chief engineer, Washington Roebling, was paralyzed by it. His wife, Emily Warren Roebling, carried his orders to the site and ran much of the job for the next 11 years.

In other words, it's the scuba diver's nightmare, on a construction site.

5. 🚇 The 4 and 5 trains run through a tunnel dug inside squeezed air

The Joralemon Street Tunnel opened on January 9, 1908, as the first subway tunnel between Manhattan and Brooklyn. Today the 4 and 5 trains use it, dipping 91 to 95 feet (about 28 m) below the river's high-water line.

Here's how they dug it. A tunneling shield, a giant steel cylinder about 17 feet (5 m) across, was pushed forward 5 to 12 feet a day. Right behind it, workers bolted cast-iron rings together to build the tube.

The trick was the air. Each dig was sealed off with thick brick walls and air locks, and air was pumped in under pressure. That pressure pushed back against the river water and soft mud trying to pour in.

And here's the danger. In March 1905, the air found a weak spot and burst out in a "blowout," shooting a worker up through the mud and into the open air. He survived.

Think of it as a balloon holding back the river while people dig inside it.

6. 🌬️ The Holland Tunnel's fans swap out all its air every 90 seconds

The Holland Tunnel, under the Hudson River between New York and New Jersey, opened in 1927 as the world's first underwater car tunnel with fans to move the air. Car exhaust carries poisonous carbon monoxide, so a long, closed tube full of cars needed fresh air pumped in.

Four ventilation towers, two of them standing right in the river, hold 84 fans. Half blow fresh air in through openings by the curbs; the other half pull dirty air out through slits in the ceiling. Together they can replace all the air in the tunnel every 90 seconds.

And here's the link: its chief engineer, Clifford Holland, had been an assistant engineer on the Joralemon Street Tunnel. He died in 1924, three years before it opened, and the tunnel carries his name.

It's a bathroom fan for a tunnel under a river.

7. 🏙️ The Empire State Building rose four and a half floors a week

The first steel went up on April 1, 1930. Builders kept up a pace of four and a half floors a week; the old record for a tower that tall was three and a half. In one stretch, 14 floors went up in 10 working days.

How? Windows and other parts were mass-produced, more than 200 trucks dropped off materials every day, and cranes lifted steel straight from the trucks into place. Some upper floors were still being designed while the lower ones were built.

The frame was finished on April 11, 1931, just 410 days after work began and 12 days early. The last rivet was solid gold.

Basically, a 102-story tower went up in a little over a year.

8. 🧊 Hoover Dam's concrete would have taken 125 years to cool

Concrete heats up as it hardens and shrinks as it cools. Engineers worked out that if Hoover Dam were poured as one giant piece, it would take 125 years to cool, and the uneven shrinking would crack it apart.

So they poured it as a grid of separate blocks, some 50 feet (15 m) square and 5 feet (1.5 m) tall, stacked like giant building bricks. Steel pipes ran through each block. River water, then ice-cold water from a refrigeration plant, flowed through the pipes and carried the heat away.

More than 582 miles (937 km) of cooling pipe ended up buried inside. Once a block stopped shrinking, its pipes were filled with grout, a runny cement.

The builders gave the dam its own air conditioning while it set.

9. 🕸️ A safety net under the Golden Gate Bridge started the "Half Way to Hell Club"

Work on the Golden Gate Bridge began in 1933, with men working high above the water. Chief engineer Joseph Strauss tried something new: a movable safety net hung under them.

It caught 19 men during the project. They called themselves the Half Way to Hell Club.

The net couldn't stop everything. On February 17, 1937, a scaffold carrying 12 men broke loose and tore right through it. Ten died, and two survived a 200-foot (61 m) fall into the water. The bridge opened that May.

Think of it as a circus trapeze net, stretched under a bridge.

10. 🚒 Fire hoses helped squeeze in the Gateway Arch's last piece

St. Louis's 630-foot (192 m) Gateway Arch was built as two legs of stacked stainless-steel sections that had to meet exactly at the top. On October 28, 1965, crews lifted in the keystone, a 10-ton piece that filled an 8.5-foot (2.6 m) gap.

But the sun had warmed the steel, and metal grows when it's hot. The legs had swelled enough to shrink the gap by 5 inches (13 cm). So workers sprayed the south leg with fire hoses to cool it and make it shrink back.

The keystone slid in after 13 minutes with 6 inches to spare. Insurers had expected 13 workers to die building the arch; no one did.

It's the hot-water jar-lid trick, done backward with cold water on a 630-foot arch.

Fun fact

The Joralemon Street Tunnel from #5 has a secret on the Brooklyn side. 58 Joralemon Street looks like an ordinary 1847 row house, but the subway company bought it in 1907, gutted it, and turned it into a vent and emergency exit for the tunnel below. People call it "the world's only Greek Revival subway ventilator." London has its own fake house fronts at 23–24 Leinster Gardens, which makes you wonder how many other "houses" are really hiding something.
