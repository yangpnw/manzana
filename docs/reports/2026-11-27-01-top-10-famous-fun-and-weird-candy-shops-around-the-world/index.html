🌍 Top 10 Famous, Fun and Weird Candy Shops Around the World

Nov 27, 2026

1. 🏴󠁧󠁢󠁥󠁮󠁧󠁿 London's M&M's World is the biggest candy store on Earth

M&M's World opened in London's Leicester Square on June 13, 2011. At about 35,000 square feet, it's the world's largest candy store, roughly the size of 12 tennis courts.

The city wanted to turn the square into a "world class destination," copying the makeover of New York's Times Square. So a giant candy shop moved into the old Swiss Centre building.

It's not the only one. The first M&M's World opened on the Las Vegas Strip in 1997, and the New York store has a two-story "wall of chocolate" made of 72 tubes full of candy.

It's a candy store big enough to hold a tennis tournament.

2. 🇺🇸 Dylan's Candy Bar started with a Willy Wonka birthday

Dylan Lauren, the daughter of fashion designer Ralph Lauren, watched Willy Wonka & the Chocolate Factory on her sixth birthday. Years later, in 2001, she opened her own candy store in New York City.

Dylan's Candy Bar stocks about 7,000 candies from around the world. Lauren said she wanted to mix fashion, art and candy culture.

At its peak, it had more than 20 stores. Its big Upper East Side store in Manhattan closed in 2021 after money troubles, but the brand still has shops in New York and Los Angeles.

Basically, a kid's movie dream grew up into a real candy store.

3. 🇺🇸 Hershey's Chocolate World has a free ride with singing cows

Hershey, Pennsylvania, used to let visitors tour its real chocolate factory. So many people came that the tour couldn't handle them, so on June 30, 1973, Hershey opened Chocolate World instead.

Its main attraction is a free ride that shows how chocolate is made, step by step. Today the ride has singing cows, and it ends with a free Hershey's treat.

In 1988, the ride got a robot host named CP-1. It was gone within a year. Meanwhile, Chocolate World pulled in 1.4 million visitors in its first year alone.

Think of it as a theme-park ride that ends with dessert.

4. 🇺🇸 Atlantic City's taffy shops once sold candy in oyster boxes

By 1880, candy stands on the Atlantic City boardwalk in New Jersey were selling taffy, a soft, chewy candy. One popular story says a storm splashed seawater over a taffy stand, so the owner sold it as "salt water taffy." That story is probably made up.

Joseph Fralinger, a former glassblower and fish seller, took over a taffy stand in 1884. He had an idea: sell taffy in one-pound boxes as a souvenir.

He bought 200 boxes for his first weekend, and they sold out by Sunday noon. So he ran to a nearby seafood restaurant and finished the day selling taffy in oyster boxes. Taffy shops from the 1800s still sell it on the boardwalk today.

In other words, it's a beach souvenir named after a storm that probably never happened.

5. 🏴󠁧󠁢󠁥󠁮󠁧󠁿 England's oldest sweet shop has been open since 1827

In the small town of Pateley Bridge in North Yorkshire, there's a shop called the Oldest Sweet Shop in England. It opened in 1827, so next year it turns 200.

The building is even older. It was built in 1661, so it was already 166 years old when the sweet shop opened.

In 2014, Guinness World Records listed it as the sweet shop that has been open the longest without stopping. It still sells old-fashioned treats like aniseed balls, liquorice and sherbet flying saucers.

That's a sweet shop that has served about eight generations of kids.

6. 🇸🇪 A widow's peppermint sticks turned a Swedish town into candy land

In 1858, Amalia Eriksson's husband died just days after she gave birth, leaving her alone with a baby daughter. Back then, it was very hard for women in Sweden to start a business. But towns gave poor widows special permission, so on January 10, 1859, the town of Gränna let her sell sweets.

She made polkagris, red and white peppermint candy sticks. The name means "polka pig": polka is a twirly dance, and "pig" was old slang for candy. The sugar dough is boiled, pulled and twisted by hand, and she kept the recipe secret until she died at 99.

Today Gränna has only about 2,500 people but gets over a million visitors a year, and about 20 shops sell polkagris there.

Basically, one woman's candy turned a whole town into a candy shop.

7. 🇦🇹 Vienna's Demel fought a hotel in court over a chocolate cake

Demel opened in Vienna in 1786. In 1874, the emperor made it an official sweet maker for the royal court, and Empress Elisabeth, known as Sisi, was a customer.

Its waitresses still talk to customers in an old, super-polite style. Instead of "Have you chosen?" they ask something like "Has one already chosen?"

Then came the cake fight. The Hotel Sacher and Demel both sold a chocolate cake called Sachertorte, and in 1954 the hotel sued. They argued for seven years over things like the name and a layer of jam. In 1963, the hotel got the words "The Original Sachertorte," and Demel got a triangle-shaped chocolate seal.

Think of it as two bakeries in a seven-year tug-of-war over one cake.

8. 🇹🇷 Istanbul's Hacı Bekir says it invented Turkish delight in 1777

In 1777, a sweet maker named Bekir opened a small candy shop in Istanbul. After he made the pilgrimage to Mecca, people started calling him "Hacı," a title for someone who has made that trip.

The shop says Bekir created modern Turkish delight, or lokum, a soft jelly cube dusted in sugar. The shop's story is that he swapped flour for starch, which made it smooth and chewy. Historians aren't so sure: similar Persian sweets are centuries older, and there's no real proof he invented it.

Either way, the Ottoman palace bought sweets from the shop, and Bekir was named the palace's chief candy maker. His family is now in its fifth generation running the business.

In other words, it's a shop that's been bragging about one candy for almost 250 years.

9. 🇧🇷 Rio's Confeitaria Colombo looks like a palace made of mirrors

Two Portuguese immigrants opened Confeitaria Colombo, a sweet shop and café, in downtown Rio de Janeiro in 1894.

Between 1912 and 1918, they filled it with giant crystal mirrors shipped from Antwerp, in Belgium, framed in carved rosewood. In 1922 they added a second floor with a tearoom, and a hole in the ground floor ceiling lets you look up at its stained-glass skylight.

So many famous writers and artists hung out there that people called it "a branch of the Brazilian Academy of Letters." Presidents, a Belgian king and Queen Elizabeth II have visited too.

It's a pastry shop dressed up like a ballroom.

10. 🇯🇵 Japan's dagashiya sell "worthless" candy to kids

Dagashi are cheap Japanese candies and snacks, and a dagashiya is a little shop that sells them. The name roughly means "worthless snacks." Each one costs about 10 to 200 yen, cheap enough for a kid's pocket money.

They're designed for kids. Bright wrappers with mascots help kids who can't read yet pick their favorite, and some come with a small toy or a chance to win another snack for free. One, Fue Ramune, is a candy you can whistle with.

From the 1950s to the early 1980s, dagashiya were the place to hang out after school. When kids had more money to spend in the late 1980s, many were replaced by convenience stores.

So it's a candy shop sized for a kid's allowance.

Fun fact

The cake that Demel and the Hotel Sacher fought over may have been invented by a teenager. One story says Franz Sacher created the Sachertorte in 1832 for Prince Metternich, when Sacher was just 15 or 16 and still learning to cook. Nobody has written proof of that date, but his son Eduard backed it up years later. Foods invented by kids and teenagers could be a sweet theme to explore next.
