🌍 Top 10 Unusual Bridges

Oct 28, 2026

1. 🇮🇳 In Meghalaya, people grow bridges out of living tree roots

In the hills of Meghalaya, India, the Khasi and Jaiñtia peoples don't build some of their bridges. They grow them. They take the dangling roots of rubber fig trees and guide them across a stream, tying and twisting young roots together until they grow into each other.

Because the tree is alive, the roots keep getting thicker, so the bridge gets stronger every year. A grown-up root bridge can hold 50 or more people at once and last several hundred years. In 2017, researchers mapped 75 of them.

Basically, a bridge that gets stronger as it gets older, instead of wearing out.

2. 🇵🇪 Peru's Q'eswachaka grass bridge is rebuilt every single year

Q'eswachaka is the last Inca rope bridge still standing, and it's made of grass. It hangs over the Apurímac River in southern Peru, even though there's a modern bridge nearby.

Every year, in the second week of June, families from four nearby villages each twist grass into ropes. At the river, the ropes are braided into thick cables, others weave mats for the floor, and everyone rebuilds the bridge together. In Inca times, this work was a kind of tax. Today people say they do it to honor their ancestors and Pachamama (Mother Earth).

When the pandemic stopped the yearly work, the bridge fell down in March 2021. It was back up within weeks.

A bridge with a yearly birthday, where the whole neighborhood shows up to rebuild it.

3. 🇩🇪 Germany's Magdeburg Water Bridge lets ships sail over a river

Near Magdeburg, Germany, two canals meet at the Elbe River. Instead of making big cargo ships go down into the river and climb back out, engineers built a giant water-filled channel that carries the ships over the river. It's the largest bridge of its kind in Europe.

The idea dates back to the early 1900s, but World War II stopped the work in 1942, and then Germany was split for decades. After the country reunited, building started in 1998 and took six years.

It used 68,000 cubic meters of concrete, enough to fill about 27 Olympic swimming pools.

Think of it as an overpass for boats, with a river running underneath.

4. 🏴󠁧󠁢󠁥󠁮󠁧󠁿 Gateshead's Millennium Bridge tilts like a blinking eye

This walking and biking bridge over the River Tyne, between Gateshead and Newcastle in England, opened in 2001 as the first tilting bridge ever built. When a boat needs to pass, six hydraulic arms tip the whole bridge back. The arch goes down, the path goes up, and boats get 25 meters of space, as tall as an 8-story building.

It moves the entire 850,000 kg bridge, as heavy as nearly 600 cars, in around four minutes. Because of how it looks while turning, people call it the "Blinking Eye Bridge."

And here's a bonus: every tilt also cleans it. Trash on the path rolls down into traps at each end.

In other words, a bridge that tidies itself with a slow wink.

5. 🇳🇱 The Moses Bridge lets you walk through a moat, not over it

Fort de Roovere in the Netherlands is an old earthen fort, part of a 1600s defense line that used water to keep enemies out. When the moat was dug out again in 2010, the architects felt that a bridge over it would spoil the old fort's design.

So they built a bridge that sits down inside the moat. The water comes right up to both sides, and you walk through the middle at the water's level.

It's called the Moses Bridge because it looks like it splits the water in two, like Moses parting the sea in the Bible story.

It's a walk down the middle of a swimming pool that forgot to fill in.

6. 🇨🇳 The world's longest bridge is almost four marathons long

The Danyang–Kunshan Grand Bridge carries the high-speed train between Beijing and Shanghai. It's 164.8 kilometers long. That's nearly four marathons back to back, and since 2011 it has been the world's longest bridge in Guinness World Records.

It runs across flat land full of rice paddies, canals, rivers and lakes, including a 9-kilometer stretch over open water at Yangcheng Lake.

About 10,000 people built it in four years, and it cost around $8.5 billion.

It's less a bridge than a railway on stilts that just keeps going.

7. 🇻🇳 Vietnam's Golden Bridge is held up by two giant hands

High in the Bà Nà Hills near Da Nang, Vietnam, a 150-meter walkway (about one and a half soccer fields long) curves almost all the way back around itself. It links a cable car station to a garden, so visitors don't have to climb a steep slope.

Two huge hands seem to hold the golden bridge up from below. They look like old stone, but they're made of fiberglass and wire mesh. The whole thing took less than a year to build and opened in June 2018.

Think of it as a gold ribbon resting in a giant's palms.

8. 🇮🇹 Florence's Ponte Vecchio has shops built right on it

The Ponte Vecchio ("Old Bridge") in Florence, Italy, has stood over the Arno River since about 1345. Little shops line both sides, so walking across feels like walking down a street. At first, butchers, tanners and farmers worked there.

In 1565, a rule kicked out the butchers so only goldsmiths and jewelers could have shops, and that rule still holds today, about 460 years later. The same year, the ruling Medici family had a covered walkway built along the top, linking the town hall to their palace across the river.

In World War II, it was the only bridge in Florence that retreating German troops didn't blow up.

Basically, a shopping street that happens to cross a river.

9. 🇮🇷 Isfahan's Khaju Bridge is a bridge, a dam and a hangout

The Khaju Bridge in Isfahan, Iran, was built around 1650 for Shah Abbas II. It's also a weir, a low wall that holds back river water. So it's a bridge and a dam at the same time.

It has 23 arches and is 133 meters long. It was also built as a place for people to meet. In the middle there's a pavilion where the shah would sit and enjoy the view. All that's left of his stone seat today are a few pieces.

In other words, a bridge, a dam and a town square rolled into one.

10. 🇺🇸 "Galloping Gertie" shook itself apart four months after opening

The Tacoma Narrows Bridge in Washington state opened on July 1, 1940. Even while it was being built, the road bounced up and down in the wind, so workers nicknamed it "Galloping Gertie."

On November 7, 1940, a 64 km/h (40 mph) wind started twisting the road: the left side dropped while the right side rose, then the other way. Each twist got bigger until the road tore apart and fell into the water, just over four months after it opened. No people died, but a cocker spaniel named Tubby, stuck in a car on the bridge, did.

The collapse was caught on film and changed how every long bridge since has been designed.

It's a bridge that danced itself to pieces.

Fun fact

The piece of Galloping Gertie that fell into Puget Sound in 1940 is still down there, and it now works as an artificial reef, a home for sea life. A new bridge opened in the same spot in 1950, even reusing the bases of the old towers and the anchors for its cables. Lots of things people sink on purpose or by accident end up as reefs, so sunken ships and other underwater homes for fish could be a great place to dive into next.
