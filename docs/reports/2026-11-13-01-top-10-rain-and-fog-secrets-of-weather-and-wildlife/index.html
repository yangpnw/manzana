🌍 Top 10 Rain and Fog Secrets of Weather and Wildlife

Nov 13, 2026

1. 🇳🇦 The Namib fog-basking beetle does a headstand to drink fog

The western half of Namibia's Namib Desert gets between 0 and 12 mm of rain a year, less than half an inch. Most of its water arrives as fog blowing in from the ocean.

This beetle usually hides in the sand at night. But on foggy nights it climbs a dune, faces into the wind and does a headstand, tipping its body about 23 degrees. Fog collects on its back → the droplets run downhill → they roll straight into its mouth.

Some beetles have been recorded taking in 34% of their body weight this way. That's like a 30 kg (66 lb) kid drinking about 10 liters in one night.

It's a water bottle that fills itself while standing on its head.

2. 🇺🇸 California's coast redwoods drink fog through their leaves

Coast redwoods grow along a thin strip of the Pacific coast, from California to southwestern Oregon. Their summers are dry, but fog rolls in from the ocean again and again.

The trees use it in three ways. Their leaves soak up fog straight from the air, their bark soaks it up too, and fog that collects on the needles drips to the ground for the roots. That fog drip can be 30% of the water a tree uses in a year.

Fog may even set their height limit. Scientists think a redwood can't grow past about 122–130 meters (400–427 ft), roughly a 40-story building, and that reaching that height takes fog.

Think of it as a tree that sips water out of the air.

3. 🇨🇱 A village in Chile got its tap water from fog nets

Northern Chile's coast is one of the driest places on Earth, but a thick fog called the camanchaca rolls in from the sea. Scientists put up tall mesh nets on El Tofo Mountain to catch it: fog droplets stick to the mesh → they run down → a trough collects the water.

The village of Chungungo below asked for a pipe down the mountain. The site grew to 94 nets feeding water to the village's 300 people. But by 2002, a report found only nine nets still standing.

The idea didn't die. Fog-catching towers near Huasco, also in Chile, now make 10,000 to 50,000 liters of water a day for farms, enough to fill a small backyard pool.

Picture it as a giant hairnet for clouds.

4. 🌫️ A fog bow is a rainbow that lost its colors

A fog bow forms like a rainbow, with the sun behind you, but it shines in fog instead of rain. Fog droplets are tiny, smaller than 0.05 mm, thinner than a human hair.

Drops that small spread the light out and smear the colors together. So a fog bow has only a faint red outer edge and a bluish inside, and often it looks completely white. That's why it's also called a white rainbow.

Sailors have their own name for it: a "sea-dog." One that shows up at night under the moon is a lunar fog bow.

Think of it as a rainbow printed in black and white.

5. 🌧️ The smell of rain is named after the blood of the gods

In 1964, two Australian scientists, Isabel Joy Bear and Richard Thomas, named the smell of rain on dry ground "petrichor." It comes from Greek: petra (stone) plus ichor, the golden fluid that ran in the veins of the Greek gods.

Here's how it works. During dry spells, oils build up in soil and rocks. When a raindrop hits the ground, it traps tiny air bubbles → the bubbles rise and pop → they fling the smell into the air.

Part of that smell is geosmin, made by soil bacteria. Your nose can catch it at 5 parts per trillion, less than one drop in an Olympic swimming pool. It's also why beets taste like dirt.

Basically, every raindrop pops a tiny bubble of earth perfume.

6. 🏴󠁧󠁢󠁥󠁮󠁧󠁿 Worm charmers trick earthworms into crawling out of the ground

Earthworms have an old nickname, "rainworm," and they stay away from dry soil. They're also very sensitive to shaking: vibrations make them crawl up to the surface. In 2008, scientists suggested why: the shaking feels like a digging mole, which eats worms.

People use this to gather fishing bait. Worm charmers wiggle a garden fork or rub a stick driven into the ground. Birds do it too: gulls tap their feet in a "seagull dance."

There's even a World Worm Charming Championship in Willaston, England, held since 1980. The record is 567 worms, raised in 2009 by a 10-year-old girl from a plot just 3 by 3 meters (10 by 10 ft). Water is banned, and every worm goes back into the ground once the birds have gone to bed.

It's knocking on the worms' front door while pretending to be a mole.

7. 🇺🇸 Spotted salamanders wait for a rainy night to march to their ponds

Spotted salamanders live underground in forests of the eastern US and Canada. In spring, after a rain and once it's warmer than 40 °F (4 °C), they climb out and walk to vernal pools, ponds that dry up often enough that no fish live in them.

Hundreds to thousands can make the trip in just one night, called the Big Night. In North Amherst, Massachusetts, they have to cross Henry Street, so the town built tunnels under the road just for them.

Their eggs hide another secret. Green algae live inside the jelly around them, making oxygen for the babies as they grow.

In other words, it's a rainy-night parade with its own underpass.

8. 🇺🇸 Couch's spadefoot toads go from egg to toad in 9 days

Couch's spadefoot lives in the deserts of the southwestern US and northern Mexico. It spends the dry season underground, sealed in a watertight cocoon, after digging in with a hard "spade" on each back foot.

When a summer thunderstorm hits, the males dig out and look for rain puddles. They call, the females come, and mating happens the very first night after the rain starts.

The puddles dry fast, so the babies have to hurry: egg → tadpole → toad in as little as 9 days. Unlike most tadpoles, they'll even eat tiny shrimp and other tadpoles.

Basically, it's a race against a drying puddle.

9. 🇺🇸 Golden-winged warblers fled a tornado storm before it arrived

The golden-winged warbler is a small songbird with a yellow cap and yellow wing patches. In April 2014, scientists in Tennessee had tracking tags on five of them.

Then the tags showed something strange. All five birds flew hundreds of miles south, leaving before a storm full of tornadoes arrived.

How did they know? Scientists think they may have heard infrasound, a rumble too low for human ears that big storms give off.

In other words, they heard the storm coming before anyone could see it.

10. 🇮🇳 Mawsynram in India once got a meter of rain in one day

Mawsynram, a town in India's Meghalaya state, gets about 11,872 mm (467 in) of rain a year on average. Stacked up, that's almost 12 meters, taller than a three-story house.

Here's why. Wet monsoon winds from the Bay of Bengal get squeezed into the Khasi Hills → the hills push the air up → it cools and dumps rain almost nonstop. On June 17, 2022, it got 1,003.2 mm in 24 hours, about waist-deep on a grown-up.

It's often called the wettest place on Earth, but two towns in Colombia, Lloró and López de Micay, report even more.

It's like living under a shower that stays on for months.

Fun fact

In October 1987, people in three towns in Gloucestershire, England (Stroud, Cirencester and Cheltenham) said heavy rain brought down dozens of tiny pink frogs. Some guessed that strong winds or waterspouts had carried frog eggs all the way north from Africa. One leading idea for these "animal rains" is a tornado over water that sucks up small creatures and drops them miles away, though no scientist has ever seen it happen. Fish, frogs and other strange things falling from the sky could be a whole new theme to explore.
