🌍 Top 10 Little-Known Animal Hunting Tricks

Nov 5, 2026

1. 🇦🇺 Australia's "firehawks" carry burning sticks to spread wildfires

In northern Australia, three birds of prey (the black kite, the whistling kite and the brown falcon) hang around grass fires. Aboriginal people have long called them firehawks, and for a good reason.

The birds grab a smoldering stick from the fire, fly off with it, and drop it into grass that hasn't burned yet, sometimes up to a kilometer away, about the length of ten soccer fields. If the grass catches, a new fire starts. Small animals run from the flames, and the birds swoop in to grab them.

A 2017 study gathered these reports from Aboriginal people and other eyewitnesses. Nobody has caught it on camera yet, and not every try works: sometimes the stick lands short or just fizzles out.

Basically, they light the bushes to shake out their snacks.

2. 🎣 Green herons go fishing with bait

Green herons are small herons from North and Central America, about 44 cm long, roughly one and a half school rulers. Most of the time they stand perfectly still at the water's edge and wait for a fish to swim close.

But some of them do something smarter. They pick up a piece of bread, a bug or even a feather and drop it on the water. A curious fish rises to nibble the bait, and the heron's beak shoots down and grabs it.

That makes the green heron one of the few birds known to use tools.

It's a bird with a fishing rod, minus the rod.

3. 🌍 The black heron turns its wings into an umbrella to fish

The black heron lives in Africa and Madagascar and stands about knee-high to a grown-up. When it hunts, it does something surprising: it throws both wings forward over its head and makes a perfect black umbrella.

That umbrella casts a patch of shade on the water. Small fish like to hide in shade, so they swim right into it, and right under the heron's beak.

They often fish together, in groups of up to 50 birds. The biggest group ever counted had 200, which must look like a field of open umbrellas.

Think of it as a bird that builds its own shady hiding spot, then waits inside it for guests.

4. 🦉 Burrowing owls lay out poop to farm beetles

Burrowing owls are small owls that live in holes in the ground across North and South America, often moving into tunnels other animals dug. During nesting season, they collect animal dung, mostly from cows, and spread it around the door of their burrow.

This isn't messy housekeeping. Dung beetles love dung, so they show up. And the owls love eating dung beetles.

So instead of flying around looking for food, the owls set out bait at their front door, and the food walks right up to them.

In other words, they've set up a snack bar on their doorstep.

5. 🕸️ The bolas spider fishes for moths with a sticky ball and a fake scent

Most orb-weaver spiders spin big round webs. The bolas spider doesn't spin a web at all. Instead, it hangs out one silk line with a sticky blob on the end, like a fishing line with a sticky hook.

Here's the trick. The spider gives off a smell that copies the scent female moths use to attract males. Male moths follow the scent, thinking they're about to meet a mate. When one flies close, the spider swings the line and snags it.

Female bolas spiders are only 10 to 20 mm long, about the size of a fingernail. By day, some look just like bird droppings, so hungry birds ignore them.

It's like fishing with perfume instead of worms.

6. 🇺🇸 Florida dolphins trap fish inside a ring of mud

In shallow water off Florida, bottlenose dolphins have a team trick called mud ring feeding. One dolphin swims fast in a circle around a school of fish, sweeping its tail along the sandy bottom.

The tail kicks up a trail of mud 5 to 11 meters long, about the length of a classroom, that curves into a ring like a net. The fish inside get confused and start jumping out of the water.

Then the dolphins lunge through the mud cloud and grab them. The whole thing takes about 17 seconds, and people first saw it in 1999.

Basically, the dolphins build a net out of mud.

7. 🇵🇭 Thresher sharks stun fish with a whip-like tail

Thresher sharks have a tail that can be as long as the rest of their body. Scientists thought they used it as a weapon. In 2010, divers at Pescador Island in the Philippines finally filmed it, and the study came out in 2013.

The shark swims up to a ball of sardines and hits the brakes with its side fins. Then it flips its long tail up over its head and cracks it down like a whip.

The slap often stuns more than one sardine at once. Then the shark eats the dizzy fish.

Think of it as a sea creature using its tail like a giant flyswatter.

8. 🫧 The star-nosed mole sniffs underwater by blowing bubbles

The star-nosed mole is a hamster-sized mole from wet lands in North America. Its nose ends in 22 pink, wiggly fingers covered with more than 25,000 tiny touch sensors. That makes it about six times more sensitive than your hand.

It's also the fastest-eating mammal ever recorded. It can touch a bug, decide it's food and gulp it down in as little as 120 milliseconds, about a tenth of a second.

And underwater, where you'd think smelling is impossible, it blows tiny air bubbles onto a scent trail and then sucks them back in, carrying the smell into its nose.

In other words, its nose works like hands, eyes and a snorkel all in one.

9. 🇺🇸 The alligator snapping turtle fishes with a fake worm on its tongue

The alligator snapping turtle lives in rivers and lakes in the southeastern United States. On the tip of its tongue grows a small pink flap shaped like a worm.

To hunt, the turtle sits perfectly still on the bottom of murky water with its mouth hanging open. The inside of its mouth is gray and blends in, so all a fish sees is the little pink "worm" wiggling.

A fish swims in to grab the worm, and the turtle's jaws slam shut with huge speed and force. Young turtles catch small fish like minnows this way.

It's a fishing lure that comes with its own trap.

10. 🏔️ The bearded vulture drops bones onto rocks to crack them open

The bearded vulture lives in high mountains in southern Europe, East Africa and Asia, and it eats almost nothing but bones. Bone makes up 70 to 90 percent of its food, more than any other animal with a backbone.

Small bones it just swallows. Bones too big to swallow it carries 50 to 150 meters into the air, about as high as a 16- to 50-story building, and drops them onto rocks. Then it spirals down to check. If the bone isn't broken enough, it goes up and tries again.

Getting it right takes practice. Young birds need up to seven years to master it. Its stomach acid is so strong that a big bone dissolves in about a day.

It's a nutcracker, but the nut is a bone and the cracker is a mountain.

Fun fact

By the end of the 1900s, only about 30 bearded vultures were left in all of Spain. In 1995, a group in Spain started a project to protect them, mainly in the Pyrenees mountains, and later brought them back to mountains where they had disappeared. After 25 years of work, the group reported that Spain had more than 1,000 bearded vultures again. It's one of many animals that came back from the brink because people stepped in.
