🌍 Top 10 Underrated Holiday Gifts for Kids in 2026

Nov 9, 2026

1. 🇺🇸 The Slinky became a hit right after Thanksgiving

Old-school toys are back on 2026 wish lists, and parents are even hunting thrift stores for Slinkys. Its story starts in 1943, when a navy engineer named Richard James watched a spring fall off a shelf and seem to "walk."

He spent about a year testing steel wire until he found a spring that really could walk down stairs. Toy stores weren't interested, so on the Tuesday after Thanksgiving 1945, he set up a little ramp at a Philadelphia department store to show it off.

The first 400 Slinkys sold out in 90 minutes, at $1 each. That's more than four a minute.

Basically, the holiday shopping rush made the Slinky famous.

2. 🇯🇵 Magna-Tiles started out in Japan as "Pythagoras"

Magna-Tiles show up on 2026 lists of the hottest screen-free toys. They're see-through plastic shapes with magnets inside, so they snap together into towers, houses and rockets.

They were first sold in Japan under the name Pythagoras, the name of a famous ancient Greek mathematician. An American salesman, Rudy Valenta, spotted them on a trip to Japan in 1996 and bought the rights.

The math-y name didn't catch on in the US, so he renamed them Magna-Tiles. Sales were slow at first, but by 2015 it seemed like almost every little kid in America had them.

It's a geometry lesson that kids think is just building a castle.

3. 🎢 Some marble runs are taller than a house

Marble run sets like GraviTrax are on 2026 screen-free gift lists. You drop a marble at the top, and gravity does the rest: it rolls down ramps, spins around funnels, and drops into the next track.

Grown-ups take this very seriously. The tallest one, the Energy Machine at the Hong Kong Science Museum, is 22 meters (72 feet) tall, about as high as a seven-story building.

The longest marble run ever, built in Switzerland in 2017, stretched almost 2.9 km (1.8 miles). That's a walk of about half an hour.

Think of it as a roller coaster where the marble is the only rider.

4. 💡 Every Lite-Brite picture can only be made once

A mini Lite-Brite is on 2026 stocking-stuffer lists. The toy first came out in 1967: a light box with black paper on the front and a pile of small colored plastic pegs.

You push the pegs through the paper. The paper blocks the light everywhere except where a peg pokes through, so the pegs glow like tiny colored bulbs.

Here's the catch: each peg punches a hole, so each sheet works only once. In 2022, Lite-Brite made it into the National Toy Hall of Fame.

In other words, it's a coloring book you color with light.

5. 🇺🇸 The hacky sack was invented by two friends in Oregon in 1972

Hacky sacks are on 2026 lists of trending stocking stuffers. It's a small, round bag filled with plastic pellets or sand, and the goal is to keep it in the air using only your feet.

Kicking games like this are very old: Japan has kemari, and Korea and China have games where you kick a feathered shuttlecock (jegichagi and jianzi). But the modern version was invented in 1972 by Mike Marshall and John Stalberger in Oregon City, Oregon, who sold it as the "Hacky Sack."

The name caught on so well that people now call any footbag a hacky sack, even though it's really a brand name.

It's like calling every tissue a Kleenex, but for a ball you kick.

6. 🇯🇵 Tamagotchi means "egg watch" in Japanese

A Tamagotchi that comes with a matching plush is predicted to be one of the top toys of 2026. It's a tiny digital pet in an egg-shaped case: you feed it, clean up after it and play with it so it grows up.

The name mixes the Japanese words tamago (egg) and uotchi (watch), because the first idea was to wear it on your wrist like a watch. It came out in Japan on November 23, 1996.

It got so popular that Bandai, the company that makes it, told its workers not to carry bags with the Bandai logo, in case someone stole them hoping for a Tamagotchi. More than 98 million have been sold.

Basically, it's a pet that lives on your keychain.

7. 🇵🇭 The word "yo-yo" probably comes from the Philippines

Yo-yos are another classic parents are hunting down again in 2026. The toy is ancient: people have written about it since 440 BC.

The word "yo-yo" probably comes from yóyo in Ilocano, a language spoken in the Philippines. In 1928, Pedro Flores, a Filipino immigrant, opened a yo-yo company in Santa Barbara, California, starting with a dozen handmade yo-yos.

Just one year later, his factories had 600 workers making 300,000 yo-yos a day. That's about three and a half every second, all day and night.

Think of it as a toy more than 2,400 years old that still hasn't gone out of style.

8. 🇭🇺 The fastest Rubik's Cube solve took 2.51 seconds

Speed cube sets are on 2026 stocking-stuffer lists. Speed cubes are puzzle cubes made to turn fast and smooth, and solving them as quickly as possible is called speedcubing.

The first Rubik's Cube was invented in 1974 by Ernő Rubik, an architecture professor in Hungary. The first speedcubing world championship was held in Budapest in 1982.

As of August 2026, the world record for one solve is 2.51 seconds, set by Xuanyi Geng from China. That's less time than it takes to tie your shoe.

Blink a couple of times, and the whole puzzle is done.

9. 🇺🇸 UNO was invented by a barber in Ohio

A tiny "World's Smallest" UNO deck is on 2026 stocking-stuffer lists. The full-size game was invented in 1971 by Merle Robbins in Reading, Ohio, near Cincinnati.

His family and friends loved it so much that the Robbins family borrowed against their house to raise $8,000 and print 5,000 copies. He sold them from his barbershop.

Later, he sold the game for $50,000, plus 10 cents for every copy sold. UNO made it into the National Toy Hall of Fame in 2018.

In other words, one of the world's favorite card games started at a barbershop counter.

10. 🌀 The fidget spinner's famous "inventor" didn't invent it

Build-your-own fidget spinners are showing up on 2026 stocking-stuffer lists. A fidget spinner has a ball bearing in the middle and weighted arms around it. Because most of the weight sits far from the center, it keeps spinning for a long time once you flick it.

When spinners went viral in 2017, big newspapers said a chemical engineer named Catherine Hettinger had invented them. But her 1993 "spinning toy" was more like a tiny finger frisbee, and it didn't have the ball bearing in the middle.

The patents for spinners with that center bearing were filed in 2016, by other people.

It's like a famous story everyone repeats that turns out to be a mix-up.

Fun fact

The Slinky didn't stay just a toy. Teachers have used it as a teaching tool in classrooms, and during the Vietnam War, soldiers stretched Slinkys out to use as radio antennas they could carry. In its first 60 years, about 300 million Slinkys were sold. It's one of many toys that ended up with surprising grown-up jobs.
