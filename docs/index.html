🌍 Top 10 Funny Phrases from Sports and Hobbies

Nov 4, 2026

1. 🎣 Getting "skunked" means you fished all day and caught nothing

Ask anglers how it went, and if they came home with zero fish, they'll say they got skunked. No skunk was involved. It just means a total shutout.

Card players use the same word. In cribbage, players race their pegs along a board to 121 points. If you get there before your opponent even reaches 91, you've skunked them, and your win counts double.

Beat them before they reach 61, and that's a double skunk.

Basically, getting skunked is losing so badly the score looks like a joke.

2. 🦃 Three strikes in a row in bowling is called a "turkey"

A strike means knocking down all 10 pins with your first ball. Two strikes in a row is a "double." Three in a row is a turkey.

The story bowlers tell goes back to the late 1800s and early 1900s. Around Thanksgiving, some bowling alleys gave a live turkey to anyone who rolled three strikes in a row. The name stuck long after the prizes stopped.

And the birds keep coming: six strikes in a row is a "wild turkey," and nine is a "golden turkey." Twelve in a row is a perfect game of 300 points.

Think of it as bowling's version of winning dinner.

3. 🏴󠁧󠁢󠁥󠁮󠁧󠁿 A "hat trick" started with a real hat in 1858

In 1858, an English cricket player named H. H. Stephenson got three batters out with three balls in a row. That's very hard to do. His fans passed around a collection, and with the money they bought him a hat.

People started calling the feat a "hat trick," and it first showed up in a newspaper in 1865. Later, soccer, hockey and rugby borrowed it for any player who scores three times in one game.

Ice hockey fans took it literally. When a player scores three goals, fans throw their own hats onto the ice. Wayne Gretzky scored 50 hat tricks in his career, the record in the NHL.

In other words, a hat trick is three of something great, plus a hat.

4. 🦆 In cricket, getting out with zero runs is a "duck"

In cricket, a batter who gets out without scoring a single run has made a duck. Get out on the very first ball, and it's a "golden duck."

The name is short for "duck's egg." A zero looks like an egg, round and empty. Back in 1866, a newspaper said that the Prince of Wales, the future King Edward VII, walked back to the royal pavilion (the players' clubhouse) "on a duck's egg."

The most famous duck happened in 1948. Australian star Don Bradman needed just 4 runs in his last match to finish his career with an average of exactly 100. Instead, he was out for zero, and his average stayed at 99.94.

It's like getting a big round egg on your report card.

5. 🇺🇸 A golf "birdie" was named after a lucky shot in 1899

In golf, every hole has a "par," the number of shots a good player is expected to need. Finishing a hole in one shot fewer than par is a birdie.

The name comes from the Atlantic City Country Club in New Jersey. As the story goes, in 1899 a golfer named George Crump hit a bird in flight with his first shot. His next shot landed just inches from the hole. His friends shouted that it was "a bird," and he finished one under par.

The three friends started calling every one-under score a "birdie." Lots of visitors played at that club, so the word spread across America.

Basically, the very first birdie really did have a bird in it.

6. 🦅 Golf scores get bigger birds the rarer they are

Two shots under par is an eagle, named because an eagle is bigger than a birdie. Three under is an "albatross," one of the largest birds in the world.

An albatross is so rare that when Gene Sarazen made one at the 1935 Masters, newspapers called it "the shot heard 'round the world." It helped him win the tournament.

Four under is a "condor." That means getting the ball in the hole with one swing on a long hole where most players need five. As of May 2023, it had been done only five times that way, and never in a professional tournament.

Think of it as a bird chart where every step up is a bigger bird and a rarer score.

7. 🏄 To "hang ten," a surfer curls all ten toes over the front of the board

"Hang ten" is one of surfing's most famous moves. The surfer walks to the very front tip of the board, called the nose, and hangs all ten toes over the edge while the wave keeps pushing them along.

It's so tricky to balance that it can usually only be done on a heavy longboard. A short board would just dip its nose and throw the surfer off.

The name caught on in other sports too. In basketball, a player who dunks and then hangs onto the hoop with all ten fingers is said to "hang ten."

It's like standing on the edge of a diving board while the diving board is moving.

8. 🌊 Riding with your right foot in front is called "goofy"

Surfers, skateboarders and snowboarders all pick a front foot. Left foot forward is called "regular." Right foot forward is called goofy, or "goofy-footed."

The popular story points to a 1937 Disney cartoon, Hawaiian Holiday. In it, Goofy tries surfing with his right foot in front, then wipes out spectacularly.

Word experts aren't so sure, though. Goofy also rides with his left foot forward in the same cartoon. The oldest known use in a surfing book is from 1962, and the phrase may have been around in Hawaii before that.

In other words, it's just which hand you write with, but for your feet.

9. 🧭 Geocachers call everyone else "muggles"

Geocaching is a worldwide treasure hunt. Players hide small boxes, post the exact location online, and other players use GPS to find them and sign the logbook inside.

The first one was hidden on May 3, 2000, in Oregon: a black bucket filled with books, videos and a can of beans. Within three days, two people had found it. Today there are over three million hidden caches around the world.

People who don't know about the game are called "muggles," a word borrowed from the Harry Potter books, which were getting popular at the same time. If a muggle finds a cache and takes or breaks it, the cache has been "muggled."

It's hide-and-seek where most of the world doesn't know it's playing.

10. 🎿 A "bluebird day" is skiing under a clear sky after fresh snow

Skiers dream of a bluebird day: a storm drops a big pile of fresh, fluffy snow overnight, and then the next morning the sky is perfectly clear and the sun is out.

Dictionaries say "bluebird day" just means a day with a cloudless blue sky, and the phrase dates back to 1860. Skiers added their own rule: it only counts if fresh snow came first. A sunny day on old, hard snow is just a sunny day.

Despite the name, you don't need to see any bluebirds. The name is about the color of the sky.

It's the snow version of a perfect beach day.

Fun fact

That very first geocache in Oregon didn't last. A lawn mower eventually wrecked the bucket and almost everything inside. Only one thing survived: the can of beans. Players turned it into a traveling game piece called the "Original Can of Beans," and today a plaque marks the spot, which makes it one of many everyday objects that became famous by accident.
