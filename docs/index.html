🌍 Top 10 Forgotten Treasures Found by Chance

Nov 7, 2026

1. 🇨🇳 Farmers digging a well found the Terracotta Army

In March 1974, farmer Yang Zhifa, five of his brothers and a neighbor were digging a well near Xi'an, China, when they hit pieces of clay figures. They had stumbled onto part of the tomb of China's first emperor, buried about 2,200 years ago.

Archaeologists then uncovered pits about 7 m (23 ft) deep, as deep as a two-story house is tall, filled with life-size clay soldiers. Estimates say the pits hold more than 8,000 soldiers, plus 130 chariots and hundreds of horses, and most are still underground.

And here's the twist: no two faces look quite alike. Workers shaped the faces in molds, then added clay by hand so each soldier looks like a different person.

Think of it as digging for water and finding a whole army instead.

2. 🇫🇷 A dog named Robot led teens to the Lascaux cave paintings

On September 12, 1940, 18-year-old Marcel Ravidat's dog, Robot, was sniffing around a hole left by a fallen tree near Montignac in southwest France. Marcel came back with three friends, and they climbed down a 15 m (50 ft) shaft, hoping it was a secret tunnel to a nearby manor house.

Instead, the walls were covered in more than 600 paintings of horses, deer and giant wild cattle. People painted them about 17,000 to 22,000 years ago.

Visitors' breath later started damaging the paintings, so the real cave closed in 1963. Today people visit exact copies built nearby.

Basically, a dog's sniff reopened an art gallery that had been closed for 17,000 years.

3. 🏴󠁧󠁢󠁥󠁮󠁧󠁿 A lost hammer led to the Hoxne Hoard

In November 1992, farmer Peter Whatling lost his hammer in a field near Hoxne, a village in Suffolk, England. He asked his friend Eric Lawes, a retired gardener with a metal detector, to help look for it.

The detector beeped, but not for the hammer. Lawes had found a buried Roman chest holding 14,865 coins and about 200 silver and gold treasures, including a silver tiger and a pepper pot shaped like a fancy lady. It had been buried about 1,600 years ago.

Instead of digging it all out, the two friends called the landowner and the police, so archaeologists could dig it up carefully the next day. The friends shared a reward of £1.75 million. And yes, the hammer turned up too. It's now in the British Museum.

It's like searching the couch for your keys and finding a pirate chest.

4. 🇪🇬 The Rosetta Stone had been used to build a fort

Centuries after it was carved, an old Egyptian stone was reused as building material for a fort near the town of Rosetta (Rashid). In July 1799, during France's invasion of Egypt, French army officer Pierre-François Bouchard found it there.

The stone repeats the same message, from 196 BC, in three kinds of writing: Egyptian hieroglyphs (picture writing), another Egyptian script called Demotic, and ancient Greek. Scholars could already read Greek, so they used it as an answer key.

In 1822, Jean-François Champollion announced that he had cracked the Egyptian writing. The stone weighs about 760 kg (1,680 lb), about as much as a cow, and it's the most visited object in the British Museum.

In other words, it was a cheat sheet that sat inside a wall for centuries.

5. 🇬🇷 Sponge divers found the Antikythera mechanism, an ancient computer

Around 1900, sponge divers (people who dive to collect sea sponges) found an ancient shipwreck 45 m (148 ft) underwater off the Greek island of Antikythera. That's about as deep as a 15-story building is tall. In 1901 they brought up a crusty lump along with statues and pottery.

In 1902, someone at the museum in Athens spotted a gear wheel sticking out of the rock. X-ray scans later showed about 30 bronze gears inside, built more than 2,000 years ago.

Turn its hand crank, and the gears showed where the Sun and Moon were, predicted eclipses decades ahead, and even kept track of when the Olympic Games came around.

It's a calendar and a space app, made of bronze.

6. 📜 Shepherds found the Dead Sea Scrolls in a desert cave

Between late 1946 and early 1947, three Bedouin shepherds were in the hills near the Dead Sea. One of them, Muhammed edh-Dhib, tumbled into a cave and found clay jars holding old scrolls.

The shepherds took a few scrolls back to camp and hung them on a tent pole while they decided what to do. They later sold three to a dealer for seven Jordanian pounds, about $28 at the time.

Those scrolls turned out to be more than 2,000 years old, and they include the oldest known copies of whole books of the Bible. By 1956, searchers had found pieces of 981 different texts in 11 caves nearby.

A 2,000-year-old library, swapped like a yard-sale find.

7. 🇮🇹 A diver spotted the Riace bronzes poking out of the sand

On August 16, 1972, Stefano Mariottini was diving to catch fish off Riace, in southern Italy. About 10 m (33 ft) down, he saw a dark shoulder sticking out of the sand and realized it was bronze.

About a meter away, he spotted a big toe and a knee. There were two life-size bronze warriors, made by Greek artists about 2,500 years ago.

He tied a buoy line to one statue's arm, covered both with sand to hide them, and reported the find. Very few big original Greek bronze statues have survived, so these two are extra rare.

Think of it as snorkeling for dinner and finding two ancient giants.

8. 🇩🇰 A girl found a golden horn, and a thief melted it

In 1639, a young farm girl named Kirsten Svendsdatter found a long horn made of gold near the village of Gallehus in southern Denmark. In 1734, a second gold horn turned up less than 20 m (65 ft) away.

The horns were about 1,600 years old. The longer one weighed 3.2 kg (7 lb) and was covered in tiny figures. The shorter one had a line of runes (old Norse letters) naming its maker, Hlewagast.

Then, in 1802, a goldsmith named Niels Heidenreich used fake keys to get to the horns, stole them and melted them down. The horns you can see in museums today are copies, made from old drawings.

In other words, a treasure found twice and lost forever.

9. 🇺🇸 A couple walking their dog found cans of gold coins

In February 2013, a couple in Northern California, known only as John and Mary, were walking their dog on their own land when Mary spotted a rusty can poking out of the dirt.

It was so heavy they guessed it held old paint. On the way home, the lid cracked open and they saw the edge of a gold coin. They kept digging and found eight cans holding 1,427 gold coins, dated 1847 to 1894.

The coins added up to $27,980 in face value, but experts said they were worth about $10 million. It's the largest find of buried gold coins ever in the United States.

Basically, the best dog walk in history.

10. 🇨🇳 A farmer's ditch led to Sanxingdui (三星堆, Sānxīngduī)

In 1927, a farmer near Guanghan in Sichuan, China, was clearing an irrigation ditch (a channel that waters fields) when he dug up a stash of jade. Then in 1986, local workers found two pits by accident, packed with thousands of gold, bronze and jade objects.

The objects are about 3,000 years old, and their style was unlike anything seen before in Chinese art: bronze faces with huge ears and eyes that pop out, a bronze tree with birds, and heads covered in gold masks.

New digs from 2020 to 2022 found six more pits, including a gold mask that's 84% gold.

It's like finding a whole lost world at the bottom of a ditch.

Fun fact

When scientists scanned the Antikythera mechanism with X-rays in 2005, they could read faint writing hidden inside the crusty lumps, words that once covered the machine's outer case. Experts say no machine with gears this complicated appeared again for about 1,500 years, until the astronomical clocks of 1300s Europe. It's one of many ancient inventions that were way ahead of their time.
