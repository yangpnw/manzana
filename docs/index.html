🌍 Top 10 Amazing Bird Nests and How Birds Build Them

Oct 29, 2026

1. 🌍 A village weaver knits his nest from 300 leaf strips

In much of Africa south of the Sahara, the male village weaver tears long strips off palm leaves and tall grasses and flies them back one at a time. One nest takes about 300 strips.

He starts with a ring. He stands with one foot on each of two hanging twigs and weaves a loop between them. Then he keeps adding to the loop in front of his head until it curves into a half-ball: that's the room where the eggs will go.

The weaving itself is real handwork. He grabs a strip near its end, folds it back, lets go, moves his head to the other side of the wall, grabs it again and pokes it through a hole. He keeps going back and forth until the whole strip is used up. The finished nest is about 15 cm long, with the door at the bottom, and up to a hundred of them can hang from one tree.

In other words, he's knitting a house with only his beak and his feet.

2. 🇮🇳 A baya weaver adds the front door only after a female says yes

Baya weavers live across India and Southeast Asia. The male cuts strips of rice leaves, grass and palm fronds, each 20 to 60 cm long, and makes up to 500 trips to finish one nest. The nest often hangs from a thorny tree over water, where hunters can't easily reach it.

First he builds a half-finished shape called the "helmet," which takes about eight days. Then he hangs from it, flapping his wings and calling to passing females. A female flies in and checks it out. If she likes it, he finishes the job by adding a long tube that hangs down from the bottom as the entrance. The whole nest takes about 18 days.

Blobs of mud get pressed onto the inside walls too. One idea is that the extra weight helps keep the nest steady in strong wind.

Think of it as a show home: the builder only adds the front door once someone signs.

3. 🥿 In Central Europe, penduline tit nests were once worn as kids' slippers

The Eurasian penduline tit is a tiny bird that hangs its nest from the very tip of a thin willow or birch branch, often right over water. The nest is a soft pouch about 25 cm tall, a bit shorter than a sheet of printer paper.

Penduline tits build with spider web, wool, animal hair and soft plant fluff. They work it all into a thick, soft bag. It's so cozy that people in Central Europe once used these nests as slippers for their kids.

And here's the twist. Their cousins in Africa add a fake door. It leads to an empty room, while the real way in is a hidden flap. The parent closes the flap behind it, and sticky spider web seals it shut.

Basically, a woolly sock that hangs from a tree, with a secret entrance.

4. 🍜 The edible-nest swiftlet builds its whole nest out of spit

The edible-nest swiftlet of Southeast Asia doesn't use twigs or grass at all. Its nest is made only of its own saliva, which it lays down in thin strands that harden as they dry.

The male builds it over about 35 days, layer by layer, gluing it straight onto a cave wall like a little shelf. In the pitch-dark caves, the birds find their way with clicking sounds and listen for the echoes, the way bats do. A finished nest is about 6 cm across and weighs about 14 grams, roughly as much as three U.S. nickels.

People harvest these nests to make bird's nest soup (燕窝, yànwō). In Indonesia and Malaysia, people even turn empty houses into swiftlet homes and put speakers on the roof that play swiftlet calls to lure the birds in.

It's a house made of spit, and people eat it as soup.

5. 🇨🇷 Oropendola nests hang down like sacks as long as a grown-up

In the tropical lowlands from southeastern Mexico to central Panama, Montezuma oropendolas live in colonies. Only the females build, weaving fibers and vines into hanging bags 60 to 180 cm long. The longest ones are as long as an adult is tall.

The nests hang from trees that can be 30 meters high, about as tall as a 10-story building. A colony usually has about 30 nests, but one had 172.

While the females build and sit on eggs, the males fight each other. The females handle the guard work too: when a giant cowbird (a bird that tries to sneak its eggs into other birds' nests) comes near, they attack it and chase it off.

Picture a tree hung with dozens of woven sleeping bags.

6. 🌍 The hamerkop's nest is so strong a person can stand on it

The hamerkop is a brown water bird from Africa, Madagascar and Arabia. Its name means "hammerhead" in Afrikaans, because of the shape of its head. Its nest can be more than 1.5 meters across and strong enough to hold a grown man's weight.

A pair builds it together. First they make a platform of sticks held together with mud. Then they add walls and a domed roof. The door is at the bottom and is plastered with mud, and it opens into a hallway up to 60 cm long that leads to the nest room.

One researcher figured a nest takes about 8,000 sticks or bunches of grass, and it can take 10 to 14 weeks to build. And a pair builds three to five nests a year, whether they're raising chicks or not. Owls sometimes move into the empty ones.

Think of it as a hut with a hallway, built by a couple who can't stop building.

7. 🪶 The long-tailed tit's nest is held together with natural Velcro

The long-tailed tit is a small black-and-white bird with a long tail, found across Europe and Asia, and its nest is a stretchy bag with a small round door near the top. A typical one takes more than 6,000 pieces of moss, spider silk, lichen, feathers and spider egg cases.

The trick is in the walls. Moss has tiny leaves that act like hooks, and spider silk forms loops. Press them together and they grip, just like the two sides of Velcro.

Outside, the bird sticks on hundreds of flakes of pale lichen, so the nest blends in with the bark. Inside, it adds more than 2,000 soft feathers to keep the eggs warm.

Hooks and loops on the outside, a feather bed on the inside.

8. 🕸️ Hummingbird nests stretch as the babies grow

The female ruby-throated hummingbird of eastern North America builds her nest alone. She uses tiny bud scales, ties them together with spider silk, sticks lichen on the outside so it looks like part of the branch, and lines it with soft dandelion or thistle fluff.

The spider silk is the clever part. It's light, strong and very stretchy. While she sits on her eggs, the nest hugs her body and keeps the heat in. Then, as the chicks get bigger, the walls stretch to make room. The silk is also sticky, so it glues the nest onto the branch.

The smallest hummingbird nests are only about 2 cm across, smaller than a U.S. quarter.

In other words, a baby bed that grows along with the baby.

9. 🇺🇸 Cliff swallows build mud apartment blocks under bridges

American cliff swallows build nests shaped like gourds out of mud, each with a small round hole for a door. Swallows from the same colony fly to the same muddy spot together, scoop up little globs of mud in their beaks, and carry them back one at a time.

They pack the nests tightly side by side and stack them on top of each other, so the whole colony looks like a wall of clay pots. Nests made of sticky clay can last for years, and some colonies have more than 2,000 nests.

They used to build mostly on cliffs in western North America. Now concrete bridges and highway overpasses work just as well, so they're spreading east.

Basically, a whole apartment block made of mud bricks, built one beakful at a time.

10. 🇺🇸 A bald eagle nest in Florida weighed almost 3 tons

Bald eagles build with branches, high in big trees near water, and they come back to the same nest year after year. Every year they add new material, so the nest keeps getting bigger. Over time, one can reach 4 meters deep and weigh about 1 tonne.

The record is a nest found in Florida in 1963. It was 2.9 meters wide, 6.1 meters deep, about the height of a two-story house, and weighed about 2.7 tonnes, as much as two small cars. It's the largest tree nest ever recorded for any animal.

Most eagle nests only last about five years. Either storms knock them down, or they get so heavy that they snap the branches holding them up. One nest in the Midwest was used for at least 34 years.

A home renovation that never stops, until the tree gives up.

Fun fact

The swiftlet nests from entry 4 are one of the most expensive animal products people eat: depending on the grade, they sell for up to about $9,500 per kilogram. Red nests can sell for even more, up to $14,000 per kilogram. Scientists in Singapore found that the red color comes from gases in the air of the cave or bird house reacting with the white nest. If spit soup can cost more than silver, the world's most expensive foods, and the strange ways they're made, could be a tasty place to explore next.
