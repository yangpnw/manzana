🌍 Top 10 Soup Dumplings and Small Bites Around the World

Nov 11, 2026

1. 🇨🇳 The soup in xiaolongbao goes in as jelly

How do you get soup inside a dumpling without it leaking out? You don't pour it in. Cooks wrap the meat filling together with aspic, a meat broth cooled until it sets into a firm jelly.

In the steamer, the heat melts the jelly back into soup, sealed inside the thin skin. That's also why they used to be hard to make in summer: the jelly won't set at room temperature, so fridges made life a lot easier.

The name means "little basket bun," after the small bamboo steamer it's cooked and served in. Shanghai's version comes from Nanxiang, a village that is now part of the city.

Basically, it's soup that travels as a jelly cube.

2. 🇨🇳 Some giant soup buns are eaten with a straw

Xiaolongbao has a big cousin called tangbao, "soup bun." In Yangzhou, Jingjiang and other parts of Jiangsu, each one is so big it gets its own steamer basket.

You don't bite it first. You poke in a straw, drink the soup (made from pork jelly and sometimes crab roe), and then eat the empty bun.

Soup buns started in Kaifeng, the capital of the Song dynasty, where people bit them open and drank the soup with a spoon. They spread south after invaders took the city in 1127, and today even most Kaifeng shops serve the straw version.

It's a juice box you can eat.

3. 🇨🇳 Jiaozi were first called "tender ears"

A Chinese legend says a doctor named Zhang Zhongjing invented jiaozi about 1,800 years ago, during the Eastern Han dynasty. On a winter trip home, he saw poor people whose ears were frostbitten.

So he stewed lamb, black pepper and warming medicines, chopped it all up, wrapped it in little pieces of dough, and boiled them. He handed them out with the broth until Chinese New Year. People copied his recipe and called the dumplings "tender ears."

Later, jiaozi shaped like gold and silver ingots (old money bars) became a New Year wish for wealth, and many families still eat them at midnight on New Year's Eve.

Think of it as medicine shaped like the body part it was meant to heal.

4. 🦐 Dim sum chefs are judged by the pleats on one shrimp dumpling

Har gow is a Cantonese shrimp dumpling with a skin you can almost see through. It's said to be the dish a dim sum chef is judged on.

The rules are tough. It should have at least 7 pleats, ideally 10 or more. The skin must be thin and see-through, but strong enough not to tear when you lift it with chopsticks, and it can't stick to the paper or to the dumpling next to it. And it has to fit in one bite.

It's one of the "Four Heavenly Kings" of dim sum, along with shumai, char siu buns and egg tarts.

In other words, it's a chef's final exam you can eat in one bite.

5. 🇯🇵 A Japanese city has a statue dedicated to gyoza

Utsunomiya, a city north of Tokyo, calls itself Japan's top gyoza-eating city. It has more than 200 gyoza restaurants for about 513,000 people, roughly one for every 2,500 residents.

It started after World War II. Japanese soldiers coming home from Manchuria, in northeast China, brought dumpling recipes back with them and opened gyoza shops around town.

In 1990 the city noticed how popular they were and formed a gyoza association. Now there's a 1.5-meter (5 ft) gyoza statue outside the train station and a yearly gyoza festival in the old castle park.

Basically, the whole city became a gyoza fan club.

6. 🇬🇪 In Georgia, you leave the top of your khinkali on the plate

Khinkali are big Georgian dumplings with a twisted knot of dough on top. The meat goes in raw, so as they cook, the meat juices get trapped inside. Cooks often add warm water or broth to make them even juicier.

You eat them with your bare hands: grab the knot, bite a small hole, slurp the juice, then eat the rest. Using a fork is seen as wrong or childish, because it pokes a hole and the juice spills out.

The knot itself, called the k'udi ("tail"), is too tough to eat. You drop it on your plate, and the pile shows everyone how many you've eaten.

Think of it as a dumpling that keeps score for you.

7. 🇷🇺 Pelmeni means "ear bread"

The name pelmeni comes from the Komi and Udmurt languages of Russia, where pel'n'an' means "ear bread," because of their ear-like shape.

In Siberia, people froze them outdoors in winter as a way to store food. Hunters heading into the forest carried whole sacks of frozen pelmeni, since they keep for a long time and cook fast.

Today's store-bought pelmeni weigh about 15 grams each, about the weight of a tablespoon of sugar. They look so much like big tortellini that factories often make them on Italian pasta machines.

In other words, Siberian winter was the first freezer aisle.

8. 🇵🇱 Pierogi have their own patron saint

In Poland, Saint Hyacinth is linked to pierogi, and one story says he brought them there in the 13th century. His nickname became a saying: "Święty Jacek z pierogami!" ("Saint Hyacinth with his pierogi!") is an old Polish way of saying "holy smokes!"

Immigrants from Central and Eastern Europe brought pierogi to North America. At every Pittsburgh Pirates home baseball game, six runners in giant pierogi costumes race each other between innings.

And in the village of Glendon in Alberta, Canada, a pierogi statue on a fork stands 7.6 meters (25 ft) tall, about as tall as a two-story house.

It's a dumpling with a saint, a race team and its own giant statue.

9. 🇺🇦 Ukraine built a varenyk out of snow

Varenyky are Ukraine's half-moon dumplings, filled with things like potato, curd cheese, cabbage or sour cherries. On Christmas Eve, families often hide a coin inside one. Whoever finds it is supposed to have good luck and money in the new year.

Ukrainians love them so much that the ski town of Bukovel holds a yearly varenyky festival. In 2013, it built a giant varenyk out of snow and sent it to Guinness World Records as the biggest snow varenyk in the world.

In the city of Cherkasy, a 2006 monument shows the folk hero Cossack Mamay eating varenyky from a clay pot, with a huge varenyk behind him.

It's a snowman, except it's a dumpling.

10. 🇮🇹 A legend says tortellini are shaped like a goddess's belly button

Bologna and Modena, two Italian cities, both claim tortellini. A legend from the 1800s splits the difference: in the town of Castelfranco Emilia, right between them, the goddess Venus stays at an inn.

The innkeeper peeks through the keyhole and can only see her belly button. Stunned by her beauty, he makes a pasta in that shape. That's why tortellini are sometimes called "Venus's navel," and the town still holds a festival for the legend every year.

Each tortellino weighs only about 2 grams, lighter than a sugar cube. The bigger tortelloni weigh about 5 grams.

Italy's favorite tiny pasta is a love story you eat in broth.

Fun fact

Archaeologists found a bowl of jiaozi in Tang dynasty tombs in Turpan, in far western China. The dumplings and wontons there date to the 7th or 8th century, which makes them more than 1,200 years old. Real ancient food found by archaeologists, from tomb dumplings to old bread, is a whole world to explore next.
