🌍 Top 10 Famous Buildings That Dramatically Changed Purpose

Nov 12, 2026

1. 🇬🇷 The Parthenon was a temple, church, mosque and gunpowder store

The Parthenon in Athens was built as a temple to the goddess Athena. About 1,500 years ago it became a church to the Virgin Mary, and builders flipped it around: the front door moved to the west end so the altar could face east.

After the Ottomans took Athens in the 1400s, it became a mosque, and an old bell tower inside was built up into a minaret (the tower used for the call to prayer).

Then came the worst job of all. The Ottomans used it to store gunpowder and ammunition, and during a siege in 1687 a Venetian bomb landed on it. The blast tore the building apart, which is why it's a ruin today.

It survived 2,000 years of makeovers, then got wrecked for being a storage closet.

2. 🇮🇹 Becoming a church saved Rome's Pantheon

The Pantheon was a Roman temple to all the gods. On May 13, 609, the emperor gave it to Pope Boniface IV, who turned it into a church.

That new job saved it. Most of ancient Rome's buildings were abandoned, torn down or picked apart for materials, but the Pantheon stayed in use, so people kept looking after it. It still got robbed once: in 663, an emperor stripped its bronze roof tiles and shipped them off to Constantinople.

Today it's still the world's largest dome made of concrete with no steel inside. It's exactly as tall as it is wide, 43 meters (142 ft), about as high as a 14-story building.

Basically, having a job kept it alive.

3. 🇹🇷 Hagia Sophia has switched jobs four times

Hagia Sophia in Istanbul was finished in 537 as a Christian church, built in just five years. It stayed the world's largest church for more than 500 years.

When the Ottomans captured the city in 1453, Sultan Mehmed II ordered it turned into a mosque right away. It stayed a mosque for nearly 500 years.

In 1931 it closed for four years, then reopened in 1935 as a museum. In 2020, a Turkish court cancelled that decision, and it became a mosque again.

Think of it as a building that keeps getting a new name tag.

4. 🇪🇸 Córdoba has a cathedral built in the middle of a mosque

The Great Mosque of Córdoba, in Spain, was started in 785 and grew bigger again and again, until it was a huge forest of arches.

In 1236, King Ferdinand III of Castile took the city and made the mosque a cathedral, mostly leaving it as it was. Then in 1523, a bishop started building a whole new church right in the middle of it, even though the city council fought the plan.

King Charles V gave permission. But when he later saw the result, he's said to have complained: "You have built what you or anyone else might have built anywhere; to do so you have destroyed something that was unique in the world."

It's like building a house inside another house.

5. 🇫🇷 The Louvre started as a fortress

In 1190, King Philip II of France was about to leave for a Crusade. Before going, he ordered a wall around Paris and a fortress, the Louvre, to guard it on the river side.

Over the next centuries, kings tore down and rebuilt it into a giant palace, and they lived there on and off until the 1700s. In 1793, it opened as a museum.

The old castle never fully left. Its remains were dug up between 1983 and 1985, and today they're on display underground, below the museum.

In other words, the art sits on top of a castle.

6. 🏴󠁧󠁢󠁥󠁮󠁧󠁿 The Tower of London has been a palace, zoo, mint and prison

The Tower of London was started in 1066 as a castle. Since then it has been an armory, a treasury, a royal zoo, the place where England's coins were made, a records office and the home of the Crown Jewels.

It's best known as a prison, which is where the saying "sent to the Tower" comes from. But only seven people were executed inside its walls before the 20th-century world wars. Most executions happened on the hill outside.

Its last state prisoner was Rudolf Hess, Hitler's deputy, held for just four days in 1941.

Think of it as one castle that has been a zoo, a bank and a jail.

7. 🇺🇸 Alcatraz was a fort before it was a prison

Alcatraz is a small island in San Francisco Bay, about 22 acres, roughly the size of 17 football fields. The US Army fortified it in the 1850s, and by 1866 it had 105 cannons pointing out at the bay.

The army also used it as a military prison. In 1934, it became a federal prison, where strong currents and cold water made escape nearly impossible. It closed on March 21, 1963.

Then, in November 1969, a group of Native Americans occupied the island and stayed for more than 19 months. Its lighthouse is still the oldest working one on the US West Coast.

In other words, it went from keeping enemies out to keeping prisoners in.

8. 🇫🇷 The Musée d'Orsay was a train station

The Musée d'Orsay in Paris was built as the Gare d'Orsay, a train station finished in time for the 1900 World's Fair.

By 1970, it was approved for demolition, and a hotel was planned in its place. But France's culture minister blocked the hotel, and the station was protected as a historic monument in 1978.

It opened as an art museum in December 1986. It took six months just to hang about 2,000 paintings and set up 600 sculptures in the old train hall.

Trains out, Van Goghs in.

9. 🇦🇹 A Nazi anti-aircraft tower in Vienna is now an aquarium

During World War II, Germany built huge concrete flak towers (anti-aircraft gun towers). One in Vienna went up in just 10 months in 1943–44. It's 47 meters (154 ft) tall, about a 15-story building, and a concrete shaft with 3.5-meter (11 ft) thick walls where its radar could hide during air raids.

In 1957, aquarium volunteers moved in. At first they shared it with firefighters and had only one and a half usable floors, so they took over the empty floors one at a time. Today the Haus des Meeres holds over 10,000 sea creatures.

When a new shark tank was built in 2007, its gravel was made from old wartime concrete torn out of the tower.

It's a war bunker turned into a giant fish tank.

10. 🏴󠁧󠁢󠁥󠁮󠁧󠁿 Tate Modern is a power station that still has power inside

Tate Modern in London was Bankside Power Station, built between 1947 and 1963 with a 99-meter (325 ft) chimney. It closed in 1981 and was nearly torn down.

In 2000 it reopened as an art gallery. The giant Turbine Hall, where the power machines once stood, became an exhibition space, and its overhead crane is still there. Part of the building is still a working electrical substation, owned by a power company.

It drew 5.25 million visitors in its first year, more than double the 2.5 million that the three other Tate galleries had together the year before.

It's an art gallery sharing a roof with a power plant.

Fun fact

Germany built eight flak tower complexes in Berlin, Hamburg and Vienna. After the war, knocking many of them down turned out to be too hard. In Berlin, the British needed two tries and several tons of explosives to blow up one tower in 1947, and many others still stand today. Huge wartime structures that were too tough to destroy, and what cities did with them instead, could be a whole new theme to explore.
