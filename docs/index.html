🌍 Top 10 Weird Animal Behaviors

Nov 2, 2026

1. 🇷🇺 Walruses climb sea cliffs, then fall trying to get down

Walruses usually rest on floating sea ice between dives for clams on the seafloor. But the ice near the coast is shrinking, so they haul out (drag themselves ashore) onto beaches instead, sometimes tens of thousands at once.

In 2017, a film crew for Netflix's Our Planet filmed more than 100,000 walruses packed onto one beach in Chukotka, in Russia's far northeast. With no room left, some climbed the 80-meter cliffs above it, about as tall as a 25-story building. An adult male can weigh more than 2,000 kg.

Here's the deadly part. A walrus's eyes are made for seeing up close underwater, so on land it sees poorly. When the walruses got hungry and the others below headed back to sea, the ones on top tried to follow. The crew watched them teeter on the edge for hours, and hundreds fell to their deaths.

Not everyone agrees on why. One critic said polar bears probably chased them off the edge, but the filmmakers said their team was watching the cliffs and saw no bears driving them.

So the cliff isn't the goal. It's where a crowded, near-sighted walrus ends up when there's no ice left to rest on.

2. 🦦 Sea otters carry a rock in a pocket under their arm

Under each front leg, a sea otter has a loose flap of skin that stretches across its chest like a pocket. It stuffs food in there while diving, and sometimes a rock too. It usually picks the left pocket.

Back at the surface, the otter floats on its back, sets the rock on its chest and bangs clams and shellfish against it until they crack. To knock an abalone (a sea snail with a tough shell) off the seafloor rocks, it hammers with a stone: one was seen landing 45 blows in 15 seconds, three hits every second.

That makes the sea otter one of the few mammals that uses tools.

It's a floating picnic table with its own nutcracker.

3. 🇦🇺 Wombats poop in cubes

Australia's wombats leave poop shaped like little cubes. One wombat drops 80 to 100 pieces a night, each about 2 cm across, roughly the size of a dice, four to eight at a time.

How? Scientists think the walls of a wombat's gut aren't the same all the way around: two parts are stretchy and two are stiff. As the poop slides through, it gets pressed into corners.

Wombats pile these cubes up to mark their turf and attract a mate, and a cube can't roll away. In 2019, the scientists who figured this out, Patricia Yang and David Hu, won an Ig Nobel Prize for Physics.

Think of it as leaving notes for the neighbors, but in building blocks.

4. 🐠 Some parrotfish sleep inside a bubble of their own slime

At night, some parrotfish, like the queen parrotfish, stop on the reef and push slimy mucus out of their mouths. It spreads into a see-through bubble that wraps around the whole fish.

The bubble may hide the fish's smell from hunters. It may also work as an alarm: if a moray eel bumps the bubble, the fish feels it and gets a head start to escape.

During the day, parrotfish scrape and chew coral rock and poop out what they can't digest as sand. One humphead parrotfish can make 90 kg of sand a year, about the weight of a grown man.

Basically, it's a sleeping bag with a burglar alarm.

5. 🌌 A dung beetle steers by the Milky Way

Rolling dung beetles shape poop into a ball and push it away from the pile fast, before another beetle can steal it. To escape, they need to roll in a straight line.

One African beetle that works at night, Scarabaeus satyrus, uses the sky to do it. It can keep its line when only the Milky Way or a few clusters of bright stars are visible, which makes dung beetles the only insects known to steer by our galaxy. Another African species steers by patterns in moonlight.

And they're strong. A male of one species can pull 1,141 times its own weight, like a person pulling six double-decker buses full of people.

In other words, it's a poop pusher with a star map.

6. 🇯🇵 Japan's snow monkeys soak in hot springs

At Jigokudani Monkey Park in Nagano, snow covers the ground for four months a year, at 850 meters up. In winter, wild Japanese macaques (often called snow monkeys) climb down from the cliffs and forest and sit in the warm water of the onsen (hot springs).

It started with people. In the 1960s, the monkeys were encouraged to gather there, partly to keep them from raiding nearby farms.

Bathing in winter goes along with lower stress, and the higher-ranking females hog the warm water. These monkeys even roll snowballs, just for fun.

A spa day where the bosses get the best seats.

7. 🦒 Male giraffes fight by swinging their necks like hammers

In Africa, male giraffes settle who's boss by "necking." In the gentle version, two males lean and rub against each other, and whoever stays more upright wins.

In the serious version, they spread their front legs and swing their long necks at each other, trying to land a hit with their ossicones (the bony knobs on top of the head). The heavier the skull and the bigger the swing, the harder the blow. A duel can last more than half an hour.

Most fights end without bad injuries, but broken jaws, broken necks and even deaths have been recorded. The winners get more chances to mate.

Basically, it's a sword fight where the sword is your own neck.

8. 🐦 Frigatebirds sleep while they fly

Frigatebirds hunt over the ocean but can't land on it: their feet have little webbing and their wings aren't fully waterproof. So they stay up, riding the wind. One great frigatebird tracked by satellite in the Indian Ocean stayed in the air for two months.

So when do they sleep? Scientists found they nap in the air, usually with just one half of the brain at a time, while rising on air currents. The eye on the awake side looks where the bird is going.

All that mid-air sleep added up to less than an hour a night.

Think of it as napping with one eye on the road.

9. 🏹 Archerfish shoot bugs down with water

Archerfish live in rivers, ponds and mangroves from India and Southeast Asia to northern Australia. When one spots an insect on a leaf above the water, it pokes its lips to the surface and squeezes its gill covers, which forces water out through a groove in its mouth.

The jet is shaped so the back moves faster than the front, so it bunches into a single blob right before it hits. The fish can knock prey down from up to 3 m (10 ft) above the water, about as high as a basketball hoop, and it adjusts its aim for the way water bends light.

Young archerfish miss a lot at first and have to learn. They can even get better just by watching another fish shoot.

It's a water pistol with a built-in aim fixer.

10. 🇲🇿 Honeyguide birds answer a human call and lead people to honey

The greater honeyguide, a small bird of Africa, knows where lots of wild bees' nests are. In northern Mozambique, Yao honey hunters call to it with a special "brrrr-hmm" sound, and the birds answer.

Then the bird chatters to get their attention and flies toward a bees' nest, stopping close by. The hunters calm the bees with smoke, open the nest and take the honey. The bird gets the leftover wax, which it eats.

It really pays off. In Tanzania, honeyguides helped Hadza hunters find bee nests 560% more often, more than six times as many.

In other words, it's a bird that gives directions to honey and gets paid in wax.

Fun fact

Walruses really do fall off cliffs, but lemmings don't jump. The idea that lemmings leap off cliffs in mass suicide spread after Disney's 1958 film White Wilderness, and a Canadian TV reporter later found the scene was staged: the same small group of lemmings was brought to the Bow River near Canmore, Alberta, jostled on turntables and pushed off a cliff over and over. Even its shot of a polar bear cub sliding down an Arctic ice slope was filmed in a studio in Calgary. Famous animal myths, and where they really came from, could be a great theme to explore next.
